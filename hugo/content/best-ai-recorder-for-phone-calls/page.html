iOS blocks call-recording apps, so hardware does the job three ways: a vibration-sensing case, a bone-conduction mic, and Bluetooth-earphone capture. Here's which device does which — and the consent law to check before you press record.

Recording a phone call should be trivial. It isn’t, because Apple made it hard: iOS restricts apps from accessing the call audio stream, so the App Store has no reliable call recorders — and Android restricts it too in recent versions. Hardware recorders sidestep the platform entirely by capturing sound outside the software: through the phone’s body, through your earphones, or through a mic pressed to the glass.

The platform problem

Why apps can't record iPhone calls

iOS doesn’t expose call audio to third-party apps — there’s no supported API for the voice stream, so App Store call recorders are workarounds (conference-call bridges, speakerphone re-recording) rather than recorders. That’s why the workarounds people ask about — “how does Plaud record phone calls?” is a recurring community question — are all physical, not software.

The hardware answer reframes the problem: instead of getting inside the call, capture the audio where it physically exists — vibrating through the phone’s chassis, passing through your earphones, or leaving the speaker. Three companies built products around three of those surfaces. Android offers more built-in and app routes — PLAUD’s own guides catalog them — so the hardware workaround matters most on iPhone; pairing quirks for both platforms are covered in our iPhone & Android guide.

How we picked

Call capture is where marketing copy diverges most from physics, so we rank by capture-method truthfulness first: true call capture (magnetic-case vibration sensor or bone-conduction mic) beats placement-only acoustic capture, which beats nothing. Then platform fit — iOS has no native call-recording API, which is precisely why this hardware category exists — then 3-year cost, then consent exposure, because the same device that makes capture effortless makes evidence of non-consent equally effortless. Before recording any call: the consent check

Most US states allow one-party consent — you’re a party to your own calls. All-party states like California require everyone’s agreement, and calls crossing state lines generally trigger the stricter rule. The plain-English map is in our consent-laws guide; read it before the first recorded call, not after.

Every method, same rule

Consent before any call

Hardware solves the capture problem; it does nothing about the legal one. Recording a phone call without required consent can expose you to civil liability and, in some states, criminal penalties — and the same device that makes capture effortless makes evidence of non-consent equally effortless.

Two practical disciplines: use a recorded-line disclosure (“This call is being recorded for notes — okay to continue?”), and know the rule for every state you call into. The full map — one-party vs all-party, phone-specific rules, workplace variants — lives in recording consent laws. If your calls are sales calls, the team-policy layer matters too: the sales guide covers it.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

How does PLAUD record phone calls?
The card magnetically attaches to the back of your phone via its case, and a vibration sensor pressed against the phone's body captures both sides of the call — no call-recording app, no OS permission [SPEC]. The Note Pro builds call intelligence in, so no case is needed [SPEC].
Can you record phone calls on iPhone with an AI recorder?
Yes — that's the entire point of the hardware category. iOS blocks apps from call audio, but physical capture (vibration sensor, bone-conduction mic, or earphone pass-through) works around the restriction. Owners report the Note's case method 'works well' in handset mode [OWNER].
Is it legal to record phone calls with an AI recorder?
Depends on the states involved. Most permit one-party consent — you can record your own calls. All-party states like California require every participant’s agreement, and interstate calls generally follow the stricter rule. The map, with phone-specific detail, is in our consent-laws guide.
Which recorder is best for phone calls specifically?
PLAUD Note Pro for built-in capture with no ritual [SPEC]; the base PLAUD Note for the classic, cheapest proven case method; HiDock P1 if you take calls through Bluetooth earphones and want unlimited transcription [SPEC]; Notta Memo for multilingual calls [SPEC].
Does recording a call use my transcription minutes?
Recording, no — it's unlimited and unmetered on every major device. Transcription, yes: an hour-long call consumes 60 minutes of your allowance, and PLAUD caps single-file transcription at 5 hours [SPEC]. Call-heavy users should check the cost calculator before assuming the free tier survives their month.
Do these work for Zoom, Teams, and Meet calls too?
Not as bots — but they capture what your speakers play, and PLAUD’s desktop app handles Zoom and Teams with Bluetooth headsets per owner reports [OWNER]. The three online-meeting methods are compared in the Zoom/Teams/Meet guide.
